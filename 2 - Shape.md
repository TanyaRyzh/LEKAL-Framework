# LEKAL 2 — Shape

## Goal

The goal of this stage is to transform the structured **Idea Document** into an analyzed and validated product and business hypothesis.

At the beginning of **Shape Stage**, the initial product context is already understood. However, the available information is usually incomplete and is not yet sufficient to define a coherent product direction.

The consultant does not only organize information provided by the client. The consultant actively works with uncertainty: identifies gaps, develops hypotheses and alternatives, researches relevant information where possible, analyzes trade-offs and risks, forms reasoned recommendations, and identifies decisions that require validation.

The first result of this work is **Shape v1 Document** — the consultant's product hypothesis before client validation.

**Shape v1 Document** is visualized in Miro and reviewed with the client or relevant decision-makers.

The results of the review are incorporated into the **Shape Stage**, producing **Shape v2 Document** — the validated product hypothesis and the input for Structure.

---

## Objective

Develop a sufficiently coherent and validated product hypothesis that establishes:

- who the product is for;
- what users are trying to achieve;
- what value the product provides;
- how the product may create business value or generate revenue;
- what competing products and alternative solutions exist;
- how the product may differentiate from existing solutions;
- what capabilities belong to the MVP and later product stages;
- what external systems or services may be involved;
- what constraints may affect the product;
- what technologies may realistically support the product.

Not every uncertainty must be eliminated during **Shape Stage**.

However, uncertainty must be actively analyzed where possible and must not be silently converted into assumptions or ignored.

The result must provide a sufficiently stable product direction before defining how the product works in Structure.

---

## Input

The primary input is:

- **Idea Document**

The consultant may also use:

- information provided by the client during **Shape Stage**;
- existing product or business materials;
- market and competitor research;
- technical research;
- other relevant evidence.

**Shape Stage** is expected to expand the information collected during **Idea Stage**.

Missing information does not automatically indicate that **Idea Document** is incomplete.

---

## Core Analysis Principle

**Shape Stage** is an analysis stage.

When information is known, the consultant structures and analyzes it.

When required information is missing or uncertain, the consultant first determines whether the uncertainty can reasonably be reduced through analysis or research.

If it can:

1. Make the gap explicit.
2. Develop plausible hypotheses or alternatives.
3. Clearly distinguish hypotheses from confirmed information.
4. Gather relevant evidence where appropriate.
5. Compare the alternatives.
6. Analyze advantages, disadvantages, risks, conditions, and implications.
7. Form a reasoned recommendation or a set of preferred candidates where possible.
8. Bring the result to validation.

If the uncertainty cannot reasonably be reduced without client or stakeholder input:

1. Make the gap explicit.
2. Retain it as an assumption or open question.
3. Bring it to validation.

A hypothesis must never be presented as confirmed information.

The purpose is not to eliminate all uncertainty, but to reduce it as far as reasonably possible before asking the client to make or validate a decision.

---

## Focus Areas

**Shape Stage** analyzes the product across the following areas:

1. Customer Segments
2. Jobs to Be Done
3. Value Proposition
4. Revenue Streams
5. Competitors / Alternatives
6. Product Stages / Scope
7. Integrations
8. Constraints
9. Potential Tech Stack

Assumptions and open questions are maintained across all areas.

---

## 1. Customer Segments

Identify the customers, users, and other participants that materially affect the product hypothesis.

Separate segments when their:

- problems;
- jobs;
- expected value;
- product usage;
- buying behavior

differ significantly.

Avoid segmentation that does not materially affect the product.

Where relevant, distinguish between:

- the customer or buyer;
- the primary user;
- secondary users;
- other participants in the product flow.

If the exact segmentation is uncertain, develop plausible alternatives and analyze whether the distinction would materially change the product.

---

## 2. Jobs to Be Done

Identify what the relevant customer or user segments are trying to accomplish.

Jobs should describe the underlying need rather than product functionality.

Use the following structure where appropriate:

**When** [situation or context]  
**I want to** [motivation or job]  
**So I can** [expected outcome]

A useful test is whether the job would still exist if the proposed product did not exist.

Jobs may be refined through analysis of:

- the situation or trigger;
- the current state;
- motivation;
- desired outcome;
- existing alternatives;
- current problems or friction.

The resulting Jobs to Be Done must provide a basis for evaluating the Value Proposition and Product Scope.

---

## 3. Value Proposition

Define the value the product is expected to provide to each relevant segment.

The Value Proposition must:

- connect directly to the segment's Jobs to Be Done;
- describe meaningful outcomes or improvements;
- explain why the product would be useful;
- avoid becoming a list of product functionality.

Where several possible value propositions exist, analyze them as hypotheses rather than selecting one without justification.

The Value Proposition must later be used to validate whether the proposed MVP actually delivers the promised value.

---

## 4. Revenue Streams

Analyze how the product may generate revenue or other direct business value.

Detailed pricing design is not required at this stage.

Where the revenue model is not already established:

1. Identify plausible monetization hypotheses.
2. Analyze each hypothesis.
3. Consider advantages, disadvantages, risks, and conditions.
4. Research relevant market patterns where useful.
5. Identify who is likely to pay and what the payment may be based on.
6. Form a current recommendation where evidence allows.
7. Retain unresolved decisions for validation.

Possible dimensions may include:

- subscription;
- per project;
- per user or team member;
- usage-based pricing;
- other relevant models.

Competitor research should be used to identify market signals such as:

- who typically pays;
- how products are priced;
- whether external users consume paid seats;
- whether free or trial access is common;
- evidence that customers already pay to solve similar problems.

If monetization is not relevant to the product, state this explicitly.

---

## 5. Competitors / Alternatives

Identify and analyze relevant products and alternative ways of solving the same problem.

The analysis should include, where available:

- direct competitors;
- adjacent competitors;
- internal solutions;
- manual processes;
- combinations of existing tools;
- doing nothing.

Competitor analysis must not stop at identifying product names.

Where the market provides sufficient information, build a competitive matrix containing:

- 5–10 relevant competitors or alternatives;
- 10–20 meaningful product-level comparison characteristics.

The product hypothesis must be the first row of the matrix.

The comparison characteristics must begin with:

1. **Pricing / Monetization**
2. **Platform**

The remaining characteristics should be ordered by product importance:

- core and potentially MVP-defining characteristics further left;
- secondary and later-stage characteristics further right.

Avoid using obvious atomic functionality that does not meaningfully differentiate the products.

For each characteristic use:

- `Yes`
- `No`
- `Conditional — [short explanation]`
- `No evidence found`

Do not treat information that could not be found as evidence that a capability does not exist.

After completing the comparison, analyze the results to identify:

- direct competitors;
- adjacent competitors;
- relevant alternatives;
- market and willingness-to-pay signals;
- common market patterns;
- capabilities that appear to represent market baseline;
- areas already well covered by existing products;
- potential differentiation;
- capabilities that may support differentiation;
- candidates for MVP and later product stages.

Competitor analysis must inform the product hypothesis rather than exist as a standalone market overview.

Its findings must be used to refine Revenue Streams and Product Stages / Scope where relevant.

---

## 6. Product Stages / Scope

Define meaningful product stages based on the product hypothesis.

At minimum, distinguish between:

- MVP;
- the next meaningful product stage;
- later capabilities.

Product stages must represent meaningful increments of product value or learning rather than arbitrary releases.

Scope must be derived from the preceding analysis rather than created as an isolated feature list.

Use as inputs:

- the **Idea Document** and core interaction;
- Customer Segments;
- Jobs to Be Done;
- Value Proposition;
- Revenue Streams where relevant;
- competitor findings;
- differentiation hypotheses;
- Integrations;
- known Constraints.

### MVP

Define the smallest coherent product that can deliver the core value proposition and allow the primary users to complete the core product interaction.

The MVP must be checked as an end-to-end value loop.

Walk through the primary user journey and verify that it can reach a meaningful outcome using only MVP capabilities.

If the proposed MVP stops before the user's job is completed, identify the missing capability and evaluate whether it belongs in the MVP.

Functionality must not be omitted merely because it appears obvious or expected.

At the same time, avoid decomposing the scope into implementation-level details.

Capabilities should remain at a meaningful product level.

### Scope Validation

Validate the proposed MVP against:

#### Jobs to Be Done

Can the primary segments complete their key jobs?

#### Value Proposition

Does the MVP actually deliver the value promised by the product hypothesis?

#### Core Product Flow

Does the end-to-end interaction reach a meaningful outcome without depending on functionality assigned to a later stage?

#### Competitive Analysis

Does the MVP contain the capabilities necessary to support the current differentiation hypothesis and any relevant market baseline?

#### Constraints

Is the proposed MVP realistic within the known business, technical, organizational, time, and budget constraints?

Where the boundary is uncertain, identify alternative scope options, analyze their implications, and present a recommendation or candidates for validation.

Stop adding functionality once the core value loop and meaningful stage boundaries are sufficiently represented.

---

## 7. Integrations

Identify external systems or services that may interact with the product.

Integrations should be derived from product needs, flows, scope, and constraints rather than created as a random list of external systems.

For each relevant integration area:

1. Identify the integration need.
2. Describe its purpose in the product.
3. Identify several realistic candidate systems or providers where useful.
4. Determine whether the integration is relevant to the MVP or a later product stage.
5. Identify any constraints or uncertainty.
6. Form a recommendation where sufficient evidence exists or retain candidates for later selection.

Examples of integration areas may include:

- source systems;
- issue or ticket systems;
- email services;
- AI providers;
- messaging platforms;
- identity providers.

Do not confuse internal product mechanisms with external integrations.

At **Shape Stage**, the purpose is to understand the integration need and realistic options.

Detailed integration design and final provider selection belong to Solutions unless the choice materially affects the product hypothesis or constraints.

---

## 8. Constraints

Identify constraints that materially affect the product hypothesis or scope.

Relevant constraints may include:

### Business

- commercial limitations;
- contractual requirements;
- target market restrictions;
- business dependencies.

### Technical

- required platforms;
- existing systems;
- technology restrictions;
- compatibility requirements.

### Regulatory / Compliance

- legal requirements;
- data protection;
- industry-specific regulation;
- security or compliance requirements.

### Organizational

- available team;
- required competencies;
- stakeholder availability;
- operational limitations.

### Time / Budget

- deadlines;
- available budget;
- delivery expectations.

### Deployment / Infrastructure

- hosting requirements;
- deployment restrictions;
- data-location requirements;
- infrastructure dependencies.

Constraints should influence Product Stages / Scope and Potential Tech Stack where relevant.

---

## 9. Potential Tech Stack

Identify realistic technology candidates that could support the product.

Potential Tech Stack is required during **Shape Stage** to expose technical options, constraints, feasibility concerns, and decisions that may affect product direction.

It does not define the final technical solution.

Relevant technical areas may include:

- Frontend;
- Backend;
- Database;
- ORM / Data Access;
- File Storage;
- Hosting / Deployment;
- Authentication;
- other areas materially relevant to the product.

For each relevant technical area:

1. Identify realistic candidate technologies or platforms.
2. Keep them explicitly marked as candidates.
3. Identify relevant observations, constraints, or risks.
4. Highlight decisions that may materially affect feasibility, cost, scope, or product direction.
5. Defer detailed technical comparison and final selection to Solutions.

Do not select a technology merely because a candidate is familiar or popular.

Do not require an arbitrary number of candidates where fewer realistic options are sufficient.

Where options represent different abstraction levels — for example, a database technology versus a managed platform — make the distinction explicit rather than treating them as directly equivalent.

**Shape Stage** must not become premature architecture design.

---

## Process

### Step 1 — Review the **Idea Document**

Review the **Idea Document** and identify:

- confirmed information;
- assumptions;
- open questions;
- areas requiring additional analysis or research.

Determine where the available information is sufficient and where uncertainty must be reduced.

---

### Step 2 — Develop **Shape v1 Document**

Analyze the product across the Shape Focus Areas.

For each area:

1. Structure known information.
2. Identify gaps and uncertainty.
3. Determine whether the uncertainty can be reduced through analysis or research.
4. Develop and analyze hypotheses or alternatives where appropriate.
5. Gather relevant evidence where useful.
6. Form recommendations or candidate directions where possible.
7. Keep unresolved assumptions and open questions explicit.

Do not treat the Focus Areas as independent sections.

Continuously check relationships between them.

In particular:

- Customer Segments must inform Jobs to Be Done;
- Jobs to Be Done must inform Value Proposition;
- Value Proposition must inform Product Scope;
- competitor research should inform Revenue Streams, differentiation, and Product Scope;
- Product Scope must support the core value loop;
- Integrations must follow actual product needs;
- Constraints must influence Scope and technical candidates;
- Potential Tech Stack must remain compatible with the product hypothesis and known constraints.

The result of this step is [Shape v1 document](/templates/Shape%20Template.md).

**Shape v1 Document** represents the consultant's product hypothesis before client validation.

It must not be treated as validated.

---

### Step 3 — Review **Shape v1 Document** for Consistency

Before visualization, review **Shape v1 Document** as a complete product hypothesis.

Check that:

- relevant customer and user segments are represented;
- Jobs to Be Done describe actual user needs rather than functionality;
- Value Proposition follows from the Jobs to Be Done;
- monetization uncertainty has been analyzed rather than merely recorded;
- competitor research is sufficient to support meaningful conclusions;
- direct and adjacent competitors are distinguished where possible;
- differentiation hypotheses are explicit;
- MVP candidates follow from the preceding analysis;
- the MVP represents a complete core value loop;
- Integrations are justified by product needs;
- Potential Tech Stack contains realistic candidates without prematurely defining the solution;
- Constraints are reflected in relevant decisions;
- assumptions and open questions remain visible;
- contradictions between Focus Areas are identified and resolved where possible.

If **Shape v1 Document** is not coherent enough to support meaningful review, continue analysis before proceeding.

---

### Step 4 — Visualize **Shape v1 Document**

Transfer **Shape v1 Document** into Miro.

The visualization should make the product hypothesis easy to review, compare, and discuss with the client.

The consultant may reorganize information for clarity but must preserve its meaning.

The visualization may include, where relevant:

- Customer Segments;
- Jobs to Be Done;
- Value Proposition;
- monetization hypotheses;
- competitive matrix and conclusions;
- Product Stages / Scope;
- integration areas and candidates;
- Constraints;
- Potential Tech Stack;
- assumptions and open questions.

Visualization may reveal additional gaps, contradictions, relationships, or scope problems.

When this happens, update **Shape v1 Document** rather than treating visualization as a purely presentational activity.

Do not introduce silent assumptions or functionality while visualizing.

---

### External Validation Boundary

**Shape v1 Document** is the result of consultant and agent analysis before client validation.

After **Shape v1 Document** is created and visualized, the process pauses for external validation.

The client review is conducted by the consultant with the client or relevant decision-makers.

The agent does not conduct, simulate, or infer the result of this validation.

**Shape v1 Document** must remain explicitly unvalidated until the consultant provides the results of the external review.

The process continues only after validation feedback has been provided.

The validation results are then incorporated into **Shape v2 Document**.

Only **Shape v2 Document** may be used as input for Structure.

---

### Step 5 — Validate **Shape v1 Document**

Review the visualized **Shape v1 Document** with the client or relevant decision-makers.

During validation:

- confirm or correct known information;
- validate or reject hypotheses;
- review recommendations and alternatives;
- resolve important disagreements;
- validate Customer Segments, Jobs to Be Done, and Value Proposition;
- review monetization hypotheses where relevant;
- review competitor conclusions and differentiation hypotheses;
- validate Product Stages / Scope;
- review important Integrations and Constraints;
- review relevant technical assumptions where they materially affect the product;
- capture new information;
- identify decisions that remain unresolved.

Silence must not be treated as confirmation.

Not every uncertainty must be resolved if it does not prevent the product from moving into Structure.

---

### Step 6 — Create **Shape v2 Document**

Incorporate the external validation results into the **Shape Stage**.

When updating:

- correct inaccurate information;
- incorporate new information;
- remove or revise rejected hypotheses;
- record validated decisions;
- propagate changes across affected Focus Areas;
- update Product Scope where validation changes the product direction;
- update related assumptions, constraints, integrations, or technical candidates where necessary;
- retain unresolved assumptions and open questions explicitly;
- perform another consistency review.

The result is [Shape v2 document](/templates/Shape%20Template.md).

**Shape v2 Document** represents the product hypothesis after client validation.

If validation reveals that the original **Idea Document** was materially misunderstood, revisit the relevant parts of **Idea Document** before finalizing **Shape Stage**.

---

## Output

The output of this stage is:

- **Shape v2 Document**

**Shape v2 Document** contains:

- Customer Segments;
- Jobs to Be Done;
- Value Proposition;
- Revenue Streams;
- Competitors / Alternatives;
- Product Stages / Scope;
- Integrations;
- Constraints;
- Potential Tech Stack;
- remaining Assumptions and Open Questions.

During execution of the stage:

**Shape v1 Document**  
The consultant's product and business hypothesis before client validation.

↓

**Miro Visualization**  
The working representation used to review the hypothesis.

↓

**Client Review**  
External validation performed by the consultant with the client or relevant decision-makers.

↓

**Shape v2 Document**
The updated and validated product hypothesis.

**Shape v2 Document** is the primary input for Structure.

---

## Success Criteria

The stage is complete when:

- relevant Customer Segments are sufficiently understood;
- key Jobs to Be Done are defined;
- the Value Proposition is connected to those jobs;
- relevant monetization hypotheses have been analyzed;
- meaningful market and competitor research has been completed;
- direct and adjacent competitors are identified where possible;
- relevant market signals and baseline capabilities are understood;
- differentiation hypotheses are explicit;
- Product Stages / Scope are defined;
- the MVP represents a coherent end-to-end value loop;
- the MVP is consistent with the Jobs to Be Done and Value Proposition;
- relevant Integrations and realistic candidates are identified;
- relevant Constraints are understood;
- Potential Tech Stack candidates are identified without prematurely defining the technical solution;
- **Shape v1 Document** has been created;
- **Shape v1 Document** has been visualized;
- **Shape v1 Document** has been reviewed with the client or relevant decision-makers;
- external validation feedback has been incorporated;
- rejected or changed hypotheses have been reflected in the **Shape Stage**;
- critical contradictions have been resolved;
- remaining assumptions and open questions are explicit;
- **Shape v2 Document** provides a sufficiently stable basis for Structure.

---

## Failure Conditions

The stage is not complete when:

- the target customer or primary users remain fundamentally unclear;
- key Jobs to Be Done cannot be established;
- the Value Proposition cannot be connected to meaningful user outcomes;
- major uncertainty is merely recorded without reasonable analysis where research or hypothesis development could reduce it;
- competitor analysis is insufficient to understand the relevant market context;
- the proposed differentiation cannot be articulated where differentiation is necessary;
- the MVP is an arbitrary feature list rather than a coherent product boundary;
- the core value loop cannot be completed within the proposed MVP;
- the proposed scope contradicts the Jobs to Be Done or Value Proposition;
- critical Integrations or Constraints remain unknown;
- technical feasibility contains unresolved risks that materially affect the product hypothesis;
- major contradictions between Focus Areas remain unresolved;
- **Shape v1 Document** has not been externally validated;
- client validation results have not been incorporated;
- the agent or consultant has treated **Shape v1 Document** as validated without external review;
- there is no sufficiently stable **Shape v2 Document** to proceed to Structure.

If these conditions occur, continue analysis, gather additional information, revise the **Shape Stage**, perform or complete external validation, or revisit **Idea Stage** where necessary.
